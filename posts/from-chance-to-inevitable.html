From Chance to Inevitable: How to Create Your Own Opportunities

Opportunity Hunter · Post 3

Honestly, it all started by chance.

Someone recommended a podcast, and I gave it a listen. The guest was the founder of an AI company. I really liked some of her ideas, so out of curiosity I looked into what her company actually does. Turns out it was way too technical, far beyond my background. So I let it go.

A few days later, another post of hers showed up in my feed. One part of it said, roughly: whatever you want to do or learn, if you're willing to give it time and real focus, it'll happen. Tune out the people who say it's too late or too hard. Listen to the people who want to help you get there.

I thought that was so well said. Why not give it a try? So I went to their website and looked at the open roles. On paper, I wasn't the most obvious fit. But I still sat down and wrote what I genuinely felt, tailored my cover letter and resume to this company, and applied for one role.

No response at all.

A few days later, I still wanted to try. I found the founder's personal email. She had made it clear that she doesn't really check email. I thought, I've come this far, so why not send it?

A few more days passed. Then I got a reply.

I'm still in the interview process, and I don't know how it'll turn out. But I think the process matters, and I've learned a lot from it.

Looking back, so many of the chance moments in my life seem to trace back to one thing: cold outreach.

I used to be into startups, and back then I cold-emailed CEOs pretty often. Then I stumbled on a way to automate my cold outreach with AI, and that pulled me into learning AI. Because of AI, a whole chain of things I never saw coming has happened in my life.

To me, "from chance to inevitable" doesn't mean that one cold email guarantees you a reply or an offer. It means that when you stay curious and keep meeting new people, stepping into new fields, and learning new things, those seemingly random moments start to add up and compound. If nothing comes of it this time, there's next time, and the time after that. The opportunity you end up with might not be the one you pictured at the start at all. Every try teaches you something, or introduces you to someone, or shows you a new direction. And that shapes your next choice. I believe we can't decide when an opportunity shows up. But if we keep taking action, the good things become inevitable.

In this series, I've been talking about how to use AI to create opportunities you didn't even know existed. This time I want to use my own story to show this: even if you have no background, even if you have no experience, even if you have nothing, you really can create opportunities.

Why You Shouldn't Only Apply Through the Website

Lots of people listen to podcasts, go to events, or come across a company someone recommends. If you're genuinely interested, could you be more than just its user? Could you join it? Finding a company that excites you, on social media or in real life, is a signal worth looking into.

Especially for new grads, I don't think applying through the company website is the only way anymore. It might not even be a good way. A resume sent through a job site may never be seen by a real person. So you want as much exposure as you can get, to raise the odds that a real person sees you: cold emails, DMs on social media, and if you're there in person, just go up and talk to them.

You have to be bold. Don't worry about losing face. Step out of your comfort zone. By my own honest assessment, in that field I was exactly the kind of applicant who sinks without a trace. But if you don't send it, the result is already zero. If you do, you truly have nothing to lose.

Below, I'll break it down in the order I actually went through, from simple to deep.

01Simple: Apply Through the Website

I did this. No response. But it's the foundation for the next two levels: if your materials aren't ready, getting seen by a real person won't help.

I don't have any tricks here. Just understand the company first, read the job description (JD), and put yourself in the hiring team's shoes: what kind of candidate would I want to see?

Here's how I used AI:

  1. Have AI dig up information on the company first. I also searched on my own, then handed everything to AI and asked it to recommend roles I might fit.
  2. Pull their JDs and have AI sum up the core traits they care about most.
  3. Based on those, pick the experiences from my background that match best.

But your cover letter has to have your own thoughts in it. I genuinely found this company interesting, so I wrote from the heart. If you let AI generate the whole thing and there's nothing of you in it, it won't move anyone, even if a real person reads it. At that point, you might as well just send it to the machine.

02Advanced: Write to the Founder Directly

First, have AI find ways in beyond the website: the founder's public email, the LinkedIn of whoever leads hiring, events the company recently attended or hosted.

Say a real person actually opens your email. How do you grab her attention? How do you show you're different? If nothing stands out, even if you put it right in her hands, she won't want to spend her time replying to you. And you only have one short email to do it in, so this really takes some thought.

Here's the structure I usually use for a cold email:

1. One-line intro: who I am, what I'm doing now
2. Why I'm writing to you: one specific trigger (like which podcast episode, or which line moved me)
3. One or two experiences: not vague, with a bit of detail, but kept short; pick the ones that match the traits they care about
4. What I can bring: tie those experiences to specific value for their company or organization
5. Close: say I'd love to chat, and attach my resume and cover letter

03Deep: Find People Who Work There and Ask for a Coffee Chat

Not just the CEO. Anyone who works there counts. Reach out on LinkedIn and ask for a coffee chat.

I once heard a saying: send 100 coffee chat requests to people at the company you want, and you'll get an offer. That sounds like a bit much, but I think there's something to it. It shows you take initiative and that you really want this.

Whatever the Level: No Reply or a Rejection? You Can Still Follow Up

Hitting send is only the beginning.

Even if you get rejected, even if no one writes back, you can still follow up. If you really love the company and you've heard nothing back from either the official application or your email, I think you can still try again: keep writing follow-up emails, keep sending DMs, reach them any way you can.

It might seem a little annoying. But to me, fighting for what you want, again and again, is a good quality to have.

The same goes once you're in the interviews. Between rounds, you can follow up, send some extra materials, or show something you've made.

If I had to name it, I'd call it agency. No matter what's going on around you, if you like something and want it, you should give it everything you've got.

Even if your experience isn't that impressive, compare yourself with the passive way of doing things: sending out resumes, then waiting for the hiring process to run its course. You already stand out. You're someone who takes initiative, who follows up and adds more in every round. That says something good about who you are. And those qualities don't just help in a job search. Almost every job values them.

Follow-Up Email Structure

About a week later, reply in the same thread (so they can see your last email):
1. A one-line reminder: Last time I wrote because...
2. Something new: a project you just finished, an idea about their product, or a relevant update
3. One small ask: say, a 15-minute coffee chat

You can send one after a rejection too: thank them for their time, ask what you could have done better, and let them know you'll keep following their work.

Finally

Of course, all of this takes a lot of effort. It really eats up your time and energy. So only do it for companies you truly like and really want to join. If it's just an ordinary, take-it-or-leave-it opportunity, you might be better off putting that energy into things you love.

And I think whatever the outcome, the effort you put in is always good for you. I don't want to worry about what other people think. If I get rejected, does that make me a bad person? Obviously not. It comes back to the line I keep telling myself: 不要脸的人会获得全世界. Roughly, the people who stop worrying about losing face win the whole world. So let's stop worrying about face.

A Prompt You Can Copy Right Now

I'm really interested in [company name]. Attached are the materials I found about them, plus my resume. Please help me:
1. Recommend 2–3 roles I have the best shot at, and explain why.
2. Read the JDs for those roles (links: ...) and sum up the 3–5 core traits they care about most.
3. For each trait, pick the experience from my resume that matches best. If nothing matches, just tell me.
4. Beyond the official application, find every way in where a real person might see me: public contact info for the founder or CEO, LinkedIn profiles of hiring leads or team members, and events they've recently attended or hosted. Include a source link for each one.
